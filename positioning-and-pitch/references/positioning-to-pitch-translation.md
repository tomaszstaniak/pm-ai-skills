# Positioning to Pitch Translation

Positioning defines what you are, for whom, and why. A pitch delivers that positioning in sequence, adapted to a specific audience and context. This reference explains how to translate each of the five positioning components into the eight pitch components of the two-phase model — with worked examples across PM-specific contexts: stakeholder reviews, board updates, partner conversations, and buyer pitches.

---

## Why Translation Is the Hard Part

Most teams that have done positioning work assume the pitch follows naturally. It doesn't. Positioning is a strategic document — it describes relationships between your product, the market, and your customers. A pitch is a live conversation — it builds understanding in sequence, adapts to the audience's context, and must earn attention at each step before moving to the next.

The gap between positioning and pitch shows up in predictable ways:
- Marketing accurately reflects the positioning; the PM describes the product differently in every stakeholder meeting
- Sales uses a pitch that worked for one deal but doesn't map to the actual positioning
- The board hears a strategic story that doesn't match what customers hear
- Cross-functional partners have their own interpretation of what the product is and who it's for

The fix isn't asking everyone to "read the positioning doc." It's actively translating each positioning component into pitch elements with language, placement, and intent — and then adapting that translation for each audience context.

## The Five Components and Their Pitch Locations

| Positioning Component | What It Describes | Pitch Phase | Pitch Component | What It Becomes |
|----------------------|------------------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Competitive alternatives | What buyers/stakeholders would consider instead | Setup | Alternatives | An honest map of realistic options with genuine trade-offs |
| Unique capabilities | What your product does that alternatives cannot | Follow-Through | Differentiated Value (input) | The specific features or company capabilities that make differentiation real |
| Differentiated value | The outcomes your unique capabilities create | Follow-Through | Differentiated Value (output) | Business results, stated comparatively, that the audience can repeat |
| Best-fit customers | The segment that captures maximum value | Customization layer | All components | Which insight to open with, which proof to use, which objections to prepare for |
| Market category | The frame for understanding what you are | Setup | Market Insight + Introduction | How you describe the problem space and where you introduce your product |

## The Eight Pitch Components: Openers by Audience

The two-phase model has eight components. The three Setup components require the most audience adaptation — these opener patterns show how the same component changes shape by context:

**1. Market Insight** — a specific, experience-grounded observation about the problem space:

- For a buyer: "The pattern we see across companies in your segment is…"
- For an exec review: "The market signal we're tracking that has the biggest implications for our roadmap is…"
- For a board update: "The competitive dynamic that changed this quarter is…"

**2. Alternatives** — the realistic options, presented honestly:

- For a buyer: "Companies in your position are typically choosing between…"
- For an exec review: "The approaches we evaluated and why we chose this direction…"
- For a board update: "The strategic alternatives the team considered…"

**3. Perfect World** — the ideal solution, which sets evaluation criteria before your product enters the conversation:

- For a buyer: "Given these trade-offs, the ideal solution for your situation would…"
- For an exec review: "The outcome we should be optimizing for is…"
- For a board update: "The market opportunity we're positioning to capture requires…"

The Follow-Through components — **4. Introduction**, **5. Differentiated Value**, **6. Proof**, **7. Objection Handling**, and **8. The Ask** — deliver your story into the context the Setup created. For the Ask, be specific: buyers get a concrete action with a date; executives get a decision or resource request; boards get a strategic direction to endorse.

## Component 1: Competitive Alternatives → Setup Alternatives

### The Translation Across Contexts

Competitive alternatives aren't just competitor products. In different PM contexts, alternatives take different forms:

| PM Context | What "Alternatives" Means | How to Present Them |
|------------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Buyer pitch | Competitor products, DIY, services, do nothing | "Companies in your position are typically choosing between…" |
| Exec stakeholder review | Strategic directions, investment alternatives, build vs. buy | "The approaches we evaluated before recommending this direction…" |
| Board update | Market approaches, competitive strategies, resource allocation options | "The strategic options the team considered and why we chose this path…" |
| Partner conversation | Integration alternatives, competitive partnerships, build internally | "Your customers are currently solving this with… and here's where that breaks down" |
| Cross-functional alignment | Competing priorities, alternative approaches within the team | "We considered three ways to address this — here's why this approach won" |

### Worked Example: B2B Data Integration Platform

**From positioning:** Competitive alternatives are (1) custom-built ETL with internal engineering, (2) legacy platforms like Informatica, (3) modern cloud-native tools like Fivetran, and (4) doing nothing with manual scripts.

**For a buyer:**
> "Companies in your situation typically choose between four paths. Building internally gives you maximum control but creates an ongoing maintenance burden that grows with every new data source. Legacy platforms like Informatica are mature and your procurement team knows how to buy them, but implementation takes 6-12 months and requires dedicated infrastructure. Cloud-native tools like Fivetran are fast to start — strong for straightforward connectors — but run into friction with complex data models or compliance requirements. And continuing with scripts is genuinely an option — it's the one with the lowest upfront cost but the highest hidden cost in engineering time and data reliability."

**For an exec review:**
> "Before we committed to this approach, we evaluated three alternatives: building a data integration layer internally, which engineering estimated at 6 person-months with ongoing maintenance; purchasing Fivetran, which covers standard connectors but doesn't handle our compliance requirements without significant customization; and maintaining our current script-based approach, which costs us approximately 15 engineering hours per week in maintenance and incident response."

**For a board update:**
> "The data infrastructure market offers three strategic paths. We chose to invest in a purpose-built integration layer because the build-internally option ties up scarce engineering resources, the leading vendor solutions don't meet our compliance requirements, and the status quo is costing us measurable engineering productivity."

### Common Translation Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Turning alternatives into an attack on competitors | Audiences who sense a rigged comparison disengage | Present each alternative's genuine strengths before naming where the trade-off shows up |
| Skipping alternatives in stakeholder contexts | Executives and board members need to know you considered options | Always show your work — "we chose this because we evaluated X, Y, and Z" |
| Listing too many alternatives | More than 4 options overwhelms the audience's working memory | Consolidate to 3-4 meaningful alternatives; group similar ones |
| Presenting alternatives you didn't actually evaluate | If someone asks a follow-up question about an alternative you listed superficially, credibility collapses | Only include alternatives you can discuss substantively |

## Component 2: Unique Capabilities → Differentiated Value (Input)

### The Translation

Unique capabilities are facts — they're the input to your differentiated value claims. In the pitch, they serve as the evidence layer beneath your value claims. You don't lead with capabilities (that's a feature pitch); you lead with the value they create and reference capabilities as proof.

### The Two-Question Test Applied Across Contexts

The "so what / compared to what" test works the same way whether you're pitching to a buyer, an executive, or a board:

| Audience | "So What?" Target | "Compared to What?" Target |
|----------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Buyer | Business outcome they care about (cost, time, risk, revenue) | Specific alternative they're considering |
| Executive | Strategic outcome (market position, competitive advantage, efficiency) | Alternative strategic direction or resource allocation |
| Board | Company-level impact (growth, margin, competitive moat, risk) | Market alternatives or competitor strategies |
| Partner | Mutual value creation (customer success, market expansion) | What the partner's customers would do without the integration |

### Worked Example: PM Presenting a Product Investment

**Unique capability:** The product's real-time event processing architecture handles complex event correlations that batch-processing alternatives can't match.

**For an exec review (capability → value):**
> "Our architecture processes events in real-time — but the business reason that matters is that our customers detect and respond to issues in seconds, not hours. The three competing approaches all use batch processing with 15-60 minute windows, which means their customers discover problems from customer complaints, not from their monitoring tool. That's the differentiation we're investing in deepening."

**For a board update (capability → strategic value):**
> "Our real-time processing architecture is the moat that competitors would need 12-18 months to replicate. It's why we win 70% of competitive evaluations against the category leaders. The investment we're requesting deepens this advantage by extending real-time correlation to a new class of events."

## Component 3: Differentiated Value → Follow-Through Statement

### The Translation

Differentiated value is what the audience walks away remembering. It needs to be short enough to repeat, specific enough to be credible, and comparative enough to explain why you — not an alternative.

### Differentiated Value Statement Template

```
For [audience-relevant segment],
we're the only [market category] that [specific outcome],
because [unique capability],
which means [impact] compared to [most relevant alternative for this audience].
```

### Adapting Value Statements to PM Contexts

The same differentiated value can be expressed differently for different audiences:

**Core differentiated value:** "We reduce implementation time from six weeks to eight days because we handle the data migration — alternatives hand you documentation and a Slack channel."

| Context | Adapted Statement |
|---------|------------------|
| Buyer pitch | "You'll be live in 8 days — not because we skip steps, but because our team handles the data migration that other vendors leave to you. Their self-serve onboarding averages 6 weeks." |
| Exec review | "Our implementation advantage is the #1 reason customers cite for choosing us over alternatives — 8 days vs. 6 weeks — and it's driven by our dedicated onboarding team handling data migration. This is where our customer success investment directly drives win rate." |
| Board update | "Our 8-day implementation time vs. the industry average of 6 weeks is our strongest competitive differentiator. It reduces customer acquisition cost, accelerates time-to-revenue, and creates the referral momentum that drives 40% of our pipeline." |
| Partner conversation | "When you refer customers to us, they're live in 8 days — which means they see value from our integration before the next monthly review. That makes the partnership tangible fast." |
| Sales enablement | "Lead with the implementation story. Buyers who learn we handle migration in 8 days vs. 6 weeks self-serve immediately understand the value. The proof point: ask them how long their last SaaS implementation took." |

### The Champion Test

After delivering differentiated value in any context, ask: "Could this person explain our differentiation to someone who wasn't in the room — in 30 seconds?" If no, the statement is too complex, too technical, or too long.

- Executives need to explain it to their peers
- Board members need to reference it in strategic discussions
- Partners need to describe it to their customers
- Buyers need to advocate for it in internal evaluations

## Component 4: Best-Fit Customers → Pitch Customization Layer

### The Translation

Best-fit customer definition doesn't appear as a pitch section — it's the customization layer that determines how every pitch component is tuned for a specific audience.

### Customization Decision Framework for PMs

Before any pitch — to any audience — answer:

| Question | How Best-Fit Customer Definition Answers It |
|----------|---------------------------------------------|
| Which market insight will resonate? | Insights about the problems and patterns specific to best-fit customers |
| Which proof should I lead with? | Evidence from customers who match the audience's profile or care about the same outcomes |
| Which differentiated value claim matters most? | The claim that connects to the pain this specific audience has confirmed |
| Which objections should I prepare for? | Concerns common to this audience type or stakeholder role |
| How much Setup does this audience need? | Audiences familiar with the market need less context; new audiences need more |

### PM-Specific Customization Contexts

| Audience | What Best-Fit Customer Definition Tells You |
|----------|---------------------------------------------|
| Buyer (in best-fit segment) | Lead with segment-specific insight; use segment-matched proof; prepare for segment-typical objections |
| Buyer (outside best-fit segment) | Be honest about fit — "our product is strongest when [best-fit criteria]; here's how your situation relates" |
| Executive stakeholders | Frame best-fit customers as your strategic focus — "this is where we win, this is where we invest" |
| Board | Best-fit customer metrics (win rate, retention, expansion) are the proof that positioning is working |
| Cross-functional teams | Best-fit customer definition is the shared filter for prioritization — "we build for this profile" |

## Component 5: Market Category → Setup Framing

### The Translation

Market category choice determines two things in the pitch: (1) how you describe the problem space in the Market Insight, and (2) how you introduce your product.

### Category Type Affects Setup Depth

The more established your category, the less setup the pitch needs — established categories need only moderate framing, niches need a niche-specific problem statement, and new categories need substantial education before the product introduction lands. See the "Category Strategy and Pitch Implications" table in [market-category-strategies.md](market-category-strategies.md) for the full breakdown of setup depth, market insight focus, and introduction approach per strategy.

### Worked Example: New Category in a Board Context

**Product:** A platform that helps product teams capture, synthesize, and act on customer feedback from multiple channels (support tickets, sales calls, NPS surveys, social media).

**Rejected category:** Customer feedback tool (activates comparison with SurveyMonkey, Typeform — survey tools, not synthesis platforms).

**Rejected category:** Product analytics (activates comparison with Amplitude, Mixpanel — quantitative analytics, not qualitative feedback synthesis).

**Chosen category:** Voice of Customer intelligence platform.

**Board update (Setup with category framing):**
> "Product teams today are drowning in unstructured customer signal — support tickets, sales call transcripts, NPS verbatims, social mentions — but there's no system that synthesizes these into actionable product intelligence. Existing tools either collect feedback (survey tools like Typeform) or analyze behavior (analytics tools like Amplitude), but neither bridges the gap between what customers say and what the product team should build. That gap is what 'Voice of Customer intelligence' addresses — and we're building the defining product in this space."

**Product introduction (after Setup):**
> "Our platform ingests feedback from every channel, uses NLP to identify patterns across thousands of data points, and surfaces the product themes that matter — ranked by revenue impact and customer segment. We're not a survey tool and we're not an analytics dashboard. We're the layer that sits between customer signal and product decisions."

## The Full Translation Map: One Product, Five Contexts

To show how the same positioning translates across PM contexts, here's a complete example for a single product:

**Product:** API monitoring platform for B2B SaaS companies.

**Positioning:**
- **Competitive alternatives:** Datadog APM, custom Grafana/Prometheus setup, basic uptime monitoring (Pingdom), do nothing
- **Unique capabilities:** Auto-discovery of API dependencies without manual instrumentation; latency anomaly detection that distinguishes transient spikes from real degradation
- **Differentiated value:** Detects API performance degradation before it becomes a customer-facing incident — without requiring a dedicated SRE team to configure and maintain monitoring
- **Best-fit customers:** B2B SaaS companies, 50-500 employees, microservices architecture, no dedicated SRE team
- **Market category:** API observability (niche within observability)

### Buyer Pitch

**Setup:**
> "The pattern we see with SaaS companies at your scale is that API monitoring breaks down in a predictable way. You start with basic uptime checks, they work until you're running 20+ services, and then you get an incident that uptime monitoring missed entirely — because the API was technically 'up' but responding with 3-second latency instead of 200ms. At that point, teams usually evaluate Datadog or build something on Prometheus, both of which require a dedicated platform engineer to configure and maintain. For companies without an SRE team, that's a significant investment just to achieve monitoring baseline."

**Follow-Through:**
> "That's the specific problem we built for. We auto-discover your API topology — no manual instrumentation — and our anomaly detection distinguishes between transient spikes and real degradation patterns. The result: your engineering team catches performance issues before customers do, without needing a platform engineer to configure alerts. Companies at your stage typically go from 'finding out about API issues from customer complaints' to 'catching them internally before anyone notices' within the first week."

### Exec Stakeholder Review

**Setup:**
> "Our competitive position in the API observability space is strengthening. The observability market is consolidating around two models: enterprise-grade platforms like Datadog that require dedicated SRE teams, and DIY stacks on Prometheus that require significant engineering investment. Neither serves our best-fit segment — mid-market SaaS companies who need sophisticated monitoring without a platform team."

**Follow-Through:**
> "Our differentiation — auto-discovery and intelligent anomaly detection without manual configuration — is the #1 reason cited in win/loss interviews. We're proposing continued investment in deepening this advantage rather than broadening feature coverage to compete with Datadog on their terms."

### Board Update

**Setup:**
> "The observability market is a $30B category dominated by Datadog, but the mid-market segment — companies too sophisticated for basic monitoring, too lean for Datadog's operational overhead — is underserved. That's our strategic position."

**Follow-Through:**
> "We're winning 65% of competitive evaluations in this segment because our product requires no SRE team to deploy and maintain. This quarter we're deepening that advantage with [specific investment], which we expect to increase our win rate to 75% and reduce time-to-value from 7 days to same-day."

### Partner Conversation

**Setup:**
> "Your customers who are scaling their SaaS products are hitting a monitoring gap — they've outgrown basic uptime tools but don't have the team to operationalize Datadog. That's the trigger event where our product creates the most value."

**Follow-Through:**
> "When you integrate our API observability into your platform, your customers get monitoring that deploys in minutes without an SRE team. That means they detect performance issues before their customers do — which makes your platform more valuable because the monitoring layer 'just works.'"

## Building Proof for Positioning Claims

Proof (pitch component 6) must be matched to the audience's context:

**For stakeholder pitches (exec reviews, board updates):**
- Lead with metrics: customer adoption, win rates against specific alternatives, retention in best-fit segment
- Use customer quotes that specifically reference your differentiated value, not generic satisfaction
- Show competitive evidence: deals won against named alternatives, analyst positioning, market share in your niche

**For buyer conversations:**
- Match proof to the buyer's profile: industry, size, use case, and the specific outcome they care about
- A single deeply matched case study outweighs ten logo slides
- Live demos structured as proof of differentiation — not feature tours — are the most powerful format

**For cross-functional alignment:**
- Share win/loss data that validates or challenges positioning assumptions
- Use customer interview quotes that surface how customers describe your product versus how you describe it
- Track whether the language customers use matches your positioning language — gaps indicate positioning drift

### Product Demos as Positioning Validation

Demos should prove your positioning is true, not show everything your product does.

**Weak demo structure:** Walk through features in the order they appear in the product navigation.

**Strong demo structure:** Set up the problem (the market insight), show how alternatives handle it (their limitations), then demonstrate how your product handles it differently (your unique capability in action, producing differentiated value the audience can see).

## Positioning-to-Pitch Alignment Audit for PMs

Run this audit quarterly, or whenever you notice inconsistency between how different teams or contexts describe the product:

| Audit Question | Green | Yellow | Red |
|----------------|-------|--------|-----|
| Can every PM, marketer, and sales rep state the best-fit customer profile consistently? | Consistent across all roles | Minor variations in language | Fundamentally different descriptions |
| Does the alternatives discussion in pitches match the competitive alternatives in positioning? | Exact match | Some alternatives missing or added | No connection |
| Does the market category appear consistently across all contexts (buyer, exec, board, partner)? | Same category, adapted language | Category varies by context | Contradictory framing |
| Is every differentiated value claim traceable to a unique capability? | All claims grounded | Most grounded, some generic | Claims that can't be supported |
| Are proof assets organized by best-fit customer segments? | Yes, actively maintained | Some segmentation | One-size-fits-all proof |
| Does the pitch adapt appropriately for different audience contexts? | Adapted for each context | One pitch with minor tweaks | Same pitch for every audience |

If any row is Red, the positioning or the pitch translation needs work before the next external conversation. Yellow rows are improvement items for the next cross-functional review.
