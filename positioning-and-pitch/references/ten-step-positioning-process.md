# Ten-Step Positioning Process

Positioning is not brainstormed in a meeting — it's derived through a sequential process where each step depends on the outputs of the previous steps. This reference provides a facilitation guide with timing, participant guidance, decision-making protocols, and PM-specific considerations for each step.

---

## Process Overview

| Step | Focus | Typical Duration | Key Output |
|------|-------|-----------------|------------|
| 1 | Assemble the positioning team | Pre-work (1 week) | Confirmed participants and pre-read materials |
| 2 | Map competitive alternatives | 45–60 min | Exhaustive list of what buyers do without you |
| 3 | Identify unique capabilities | 45–60 min | Capabilities that no alternative can claim |
| 4 | Map to differentiated value | 60–90 min | Business outcomes only you deliver, stated comparatively |
| 5 | Define best-fit customers | 30–45 min | Observable characteristics of your highest-value segment |
| 6 | Choose market category | 45–60 min | The frame that makes your strengths most obvious |
| 7 | Identify relevant trends | 15–20 min | Optional: market trends that add urgency or credibility |
| 8 | Test internally | 1–2 weeks post-workshop | Cross-functional alignment validated |
| 9 | Translate to pitch | 1–2 weeks post-workshop | Positioning mapped to the two-phase pitch model |
| 10 | Commit and iterate | Ongoing (quarterly review) | Living positioning document maintained over time |

**Total workshop time (Steps 2–7):** 4–6 hours. Run as a single session if possible. Splitting across days introduces context loss and re-alignment overhead.

---

## Step 1: Assemble the Positioning Team

### Who Must Be in the Room

| Role | Why They're Essential | What They Bring |
|------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Product Manager | Facilitator and owner of the output; connects positioning to roadmap | Product strategy context, feature knowledge, customer insight |
| Product Marketing / Marketing | Translates positioning into messaging and campaigns | Market perception, competitive intelligence, analyst relationships |
| Sales (senior AE or sales leader) | Hears buyer objections and alternatives firsthand | Real-time market feedback, competitive dynamics, deal-level insight |
| Customer Success | Knows what customers actually value after buying | Retention data, adoption patterns, common expansion triggers |
| Senior leader (VP/CPO/CEO) | Can make binding decisions; prevents revisiting | Strategic context, authority to resolve disagreements |
| Engineering lead (optional but valuable) | Grounds "unique capability" claims in technical reality | Architecture knowledge, roadmap feasibility, honest assessment of uniqueness |

### Pre-Work for Participants

Distribute 1 week before the workshop:

- Brief on the [five components of positioning](five-components-of-positioning.md)
- Customer interview summaries or win/loss data relevant to positioning
- Current competitive landscape overview
- Prompt: "Come prepared to answer: what would our best customers do if we didn't exist?"

### Facilitation Ground Rules

- All claims must be grounded in evidence (customer data, market data, or direct experience) — not aspiration
- "I think" statements are prompts for investigation, not positioning inputs
- Disagreements are surfaced and resolved, not tabled — the senior leader is the tiebreaker
- The output is a working document, not a final artifact — it will be tested and revised

## Step 2: Map Competitive Alternatives

### Process

**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

**Facilitator prompt:** "If our product disappeared tomorrow, what would our customers do? Not who our competitors are — what would customers actually do to solve this problem?"

**Process:**
1. Silent brainstorm (5 min): Each participant writes alternatives on sticky notes or in a shared doc
2. Share and cluster (15 min): Group similar alternatives; ensure all types are represented (direct, adjacent, DIY, services, do nothing)
3. Validate (15 min): For each alternative, ask "Have we actually lost a deal or customer to this?" — remove theoretical alternatives that don't appear in real buyer decisions
4. Characterize (15 min): For each validated alternative, note genuine strengths and key trade-offs

**Output format:**

| Alternative | Type | Evidence (deals lost, customer mentions) | Genuine Strengths | Key Trade-offs |
|-------------|------|----------------------------------------|------------------|----------------|
| | | | | |

### PM Facilitation Notes

- Watch for the team listing competitors they want to beat rather than alternatives customers actually consider — these are different lists
- "Do nothing" always belongs on the list; push the team to quantify its cost
- If the team can't agree on alternatives, it's a signal that different team members are talking to different customer segments — surface this for Step 5
- Alternatives from sales (what they hear in deals) may differ from alternatives that customer success sees (what customers migrated from) — both are valid inputs

## Step 3: Identify Unique Capabilities

### Process

**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

**Facilitator prompt:** "For each competitive alternative, what can we do that they can't? I mean specifically — things they would need to build, acquire, or fundamentally change their approach to match."

**Process:**
1. Alternative-by-alternative review (30 min): Take each alternative from Step 2 and ask: "What specific capabilities do we have that this alternative lacks?" Record capabilities with evidence.
2. Uniqueness test (15 min): For each capability, apply the four-question test:
   - Could a competitor claim this today? → If yes, remove
   - Could they replicate it in 6 months? → If yes, flag as temporary
   - Is it a product or company capability? → Note both
   - Can we demonstrate it? → If not, flag as aspirational
3. Consolidate (10 min): Merge duplicates, remove non-unique items, flag temporary advantages

**Output format:**

| Capability | Type (Product / Company) | Alternatives It Beats | Evidence | Uniqueness Status |
|------------|------------------------|----------------------|----------|-------------------|
| | | | | Unique / Temporary / Parity |

### PM Facilitation Notes

- Engineering input is critical here — product and marketing often overestimate uniqueness; engineering knows what competitors could build quickly
- Watch for "better at X" claims — "better" is not unique; "architecturally different approach to X" might be
- Company capabilities (expertise, data sets, relationships, delivery models) are often more durable than product capabilities — don't overlook them
- If the team struggles to find unique capabilities, the positioning problem may be fundamental — you may need to rethink your product strategy, not just your messaging

---

## Step 4: Map Capabilities to Differentiated Value

### Process

**Duration:** 60–90 minutes (this is typically the longest and most important step)

**Facilitator prompt:** "For each unique capability, we're going to push through two questions: 'so what?' and 'compared to what?' — until we reach a business outcome that's specific to us."

**Process:**
1. Take each unique capability from Step 3
2. Run "so what?" chain (push 2-4 times until you reach a business outcome with an actor, an action, and a magnitude)
3. Run "compared to what?" test (ensure the outcome is genuinely different from what alternatives deliver)
4. Draft the differentiated value statement using the template:
   "For [best-fit customer], we're the only [category] that [outcome], because [capability], which means [impact] compared to [alternative]."
5. Apply the competitor-proof test: could a competitor paste this into their pitch unchanged?

**Output format:**

| Capability | After "So What?" | After "Compared to What?" | Draft Differentiated Value Statement | Competitor-Proof? |
|------------|-----------------|--------------------------|-------------------------------------|-------------------|
| | | | | Yes / No |

### PM Facilitation Notes

- This step often reveals that some "unique capabilities" don't produce meaningful differentiated value — that's fine; remove them from the positioning
- The "so what?" chain frequently stalls at the second level; push through — the business outcome is usually two more levels down
- If the team can't answer "compared to what?" it means the differentiation isn't specific enough — keep pushing
- Watch for value claims that are true but don't matter to buyers — "technically impressive but practically irrelevant" is a common trap
- Expect to emerge with 2-4 strong differentiated value claims, not 10 — narrow is better

## Step 5: Define Best-Fit Customers

### Process

**Duration:** 30–45 minutes

**Facilitator prompt:** "Given these differentiated value claims, which customer characteristics make this value disproportionately important? Who buys fastest, gets the most value, churns least, and refers most?"

**Process:**
1. Review differentiated value statements from Step 4
2. For each value claim, ask: "What type of customer would care about this 10x more than the average buyer?" — record observable characteristics
3. Cross-reference with actual customer data: your highest-retention customers, fastest-closing deals, and strongest advocates — what do they have in common?
4. Draft the best-fit customer profile using observable criteria (size, industry, team structure, tech stack, growth stage, specific situation)
5. Test with sales: "If I gave you a list of companies matching this profile, would you prioritize them above your current pipeline?"

**Output format:**

| Dimension | Criteria | Evidence (customer data) | Why This Matters for Our Differentiation |
|-----------|---------|------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Company size | | | |
| Industry | | | |
| Team structure | | | |
| Tech stack | | | |
| Growth stage / trigger | | | |
| Specific situation | | | |

### PM Facilitation Notes

- Resist the pressure to make the best-fit definition broad — "companies that want to be more efficient" is not a useful segment
- Observable characteristics are essential; you must be able to identify these customers from publicly available data or within the first minutes of a conversation
- If sales pushes back on narrowing, ask: "Which of your last 10 deals closed fastest and had the happiest outcome?" — the pattern in those deals is your best-fit definition
- This step directly feeds your discovery research plan: interview people who match this profile

## Step 6: Choose Market Category

### Process

**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

**Facilitator prompt:** "Given our differentiated value and best-fit customers, what market category makes our strengths most obvious? Where do we want buyers' minds to go when they first hear about us?"

**Process:**
1. List candidate categories (2-4 options, typically including your current category and at least one alternative)
2. For each candidate, evaluate:
   - What feature expectations does this category create?
   - Which competitors does this category activate in the buyer's mind?
   - Does our differentiation stand out in this comparison set?
   - How much buyer education is required?
3. Select the category where your differentiated value is most obvious to your best-fit customers
4. Map table-stakes features for the chosen category — any gaps become roadmap priorities or honest trade-offs

**Output format:**

| Candidate Category | Feature Expectations | Competitive Set | Differentiation Fit | Education Cost | Recommendation |
|-------------------|---------------------|-----------------|---------------------|----------------|----------------|
| | | | | | |

### PM Facilitation Notes

- Category choice has direct product implications — choosing "CRM" means you need contact management, pipeline views, and reporting; choosing "revenue intelligence" means you need conversation analytics and deal scoring
- If you're choosing a new or niche category, budget for significantly more Setup in every pitch and more educational content in marketing
- Category choice should make your strongest competitors look like adjacent solutions, not direct ones
- If the team is split between categories, test both: present the product in each category frame to a few trusted customers and see which lands better

## Step 7: Identify Relevant Trends (Optional)

### Process

**Duration:** 15–20 minutes

**Facilitator prompt:** "Are there market trends that make our positioning more urgent or credible? Only include trends that are real, relevant to our best-fit customers, and that reinforce our differentiation — not generic trends like 'AI is transforming everything.'"

**Process:**
1. List candidate trends
2. For each, test: Is it real (not hype)? Is it relevant to our best-fit customers specifically? Does it make our differentiation more urgent?
3. Select 0-2 trends that genuinely support the positioning
4. Articulate how each trend connects to your differentiated value

**PM Facilitation Notes:**

- Trends are optional — forced trend alignment weakens positioning rather than strengthening it
- A trend that every competitor can also claim ("shift to cloud") is not differentiating — it's context
- The best trends explain why your positioning is more relevant now than it was two years ago

## Step 8: Test Positioning Internally

### Process

**Duration:** 1–2 weeks post-workshop

**What to test:**
1. **Sales reps:** Can they restate the positioning in their own words? Do they believe the differentiation claims? Would they use this in their next conversation?
2. **Customer success:** Does this match what they hear from customers? Are the best-fit customer criteria consistent with who succeeds?
3. **Executives:** Does this align with company strategy? Are there upcoming product or market changes that would invalidate it?

**Testing format:**
- Present the positioning (all five components) in a 20-minute session
- Ask each person to restate the differentiated value in their own words — note where they struggle
- Ask "what's wrong with this?" — note objections and challenges
- Ask "would you use this in your next customer conversation?" — note hesitation

**Red flags:**
- Sales can't restate differentiation → language is too complex or abstract
- CS disagrees on best-fit customer → positioning may be aspirational rather than grounded
- Executives see conflict with strategy → positioning and company strategy are misaligned

## Step 9: Translate Positioning to Pitch

### Process

**Duration:** 1–2 weeks post-workshop

Map each positioning component to its pitch equivalent using the two-phase model:

| Positioning Component | Pitch Location | Translation Exercise |
|----------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| Competitive alternatives | Setup → Alternatives | Write the honest alternatives walkthrough — practice saying competitors' genuine strengths out loud |
| Unique capabilities | Follow-Through → Differentiated Value (input) | These are the facts behind your value claims — ensure every claim is grounded |
| Differentiated value | Follow-Through → Differentiated Value (output) | Draft the one-sentence claim and the expanded version; test with the competitor-proof question |
| Best-fit customers | Pitch customization layer | Map which insight, proof, and objections to use for each segment variation |
| Market category | Setup → Market Insight + Introduction | Write the market insight opener; write the product introduction that follows the Setup |

See: [positioning-to-pitch-translation.md](positioning-to-pitch-translation.md) for detailed guidance.

### PM-Specific Pitch Contexts

PMs need to pitch positioning in contexts beyond sales:

| Context | Setup Focus | Follow-Through Focus | Common Ask |
|---------|------------|---------------------|------------|
| Exec stakeholder review | Market dynamics and competitive landscape changes | How positioning informs roadmap priorities | Resource allocation or strategic direction approval |
| Board update | Market category and competitive position | Growth metrics and strategic bets | Strategic direction endorsement |
| Cross-functional alignment | Why the product wins where it wins | How each team's work reinforces differentiation | Commitment to shared positioning narrative |
| Partner conversations | Market opportunity and complementary positioning | Integration value and mutual differentiation | Partnership terms or co-marketing commitment |
| Sales enablement | Full positioning context with competitive intelligence | Pitch structure, proof library, objection handling | Adoption of the positioning-based pitch |

## Step 10: Commit and Iterate

### Quarterly Review Cadence

| Review Element | Frequency | Owner | Trigger for Off-Cycle Review |
|---------------|-----------|-------|------------------------------|
| Competitive alternatives scan | Quarterly | Product Marketing | Major competitor launch, new entrant, or market shift |
| Unique capabilities audit | Quarterly | Product Manager | Major feature ship (yours or competitor's) |
| Differentiated value test | Quarterly | Product Manager + Sales | Win rate drop or deal stall pattern change |
| Best-fit customer validation | Quarterly | Product Manager + CS | Churn pattern change or expansion in a new segment |
| Market category check | Semi-annually | Product Marketing + PM | Analyst reclassification or category emergence |

### Positioning Change Protocol

When positioning needs to change:

1. **Identify the trigger:** Which component changed and why?
2. **Assess cascading impact:** Changing one component may force changes to others (e.g., new competitor → different differentiation → different best-fit)
3. **Re-run affected steps:** Don't start from scratch unless the change is fundamental; re-run the specific steps affected
4. **Test internally** before changing external messaging — Step 8 applies to updates, not just initial positioning
5. **Coordinate rollout:** Marketing materials, sales pitch, website, and partner communications should update together, not sequentially

## Common Process Failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| No senior leader in the room | Team does excellent positioning work that gets overridden in the next exec meeting | Get VP/CPO/CEO commitment to participate and to honor the output |
| No sales input | Positioning is theoretically sound but doesn't match what buyers actually say | Include a senior salesperson who has run 50+ deals in the last year |
| Aspirational positioning | Claims describe where the product will be in 12 months, not where it is today | Enforce the "can you demonstrate this in a demo today?" test |
| Too broad, too fast | Team jumps to "we're for everyone" to avoid the discomfort of narrowing | Push through the discomfort; narrow positioning wins more deals than broad positioning |
| No follow-through on Step 10 | Positioning is done once and left to decay | PM owns the quarterly review; put it on the calendar the day the workshop ends |
| Process spread across weeks | Context is lost between sessions; participants relitigate previous steps | Run Steps 2-7 in a single workshop day; momentum matters |
