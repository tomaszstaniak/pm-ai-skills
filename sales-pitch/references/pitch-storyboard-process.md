# Pitch Storyboard Process

A pitch storyboard is the working document that assembles all eight pitch components into a coherent narrative before any slide design begins. This reference covers how to run the cross-functional storyboard session, what decisions each step requires, how to test the result, and how to know when the storyboard is ready to become a pitch.

## Why Structure Comes Before Slides

Most pitches fail at the whiteboard stage, not the presentation stage. When teams jump into slide design before resolving what the pitch says, they're making narrative decisions inside a visual tool — a format that rewards brevity and punishes nuance. Arguments about slide count, color, and layout mask unresolved disagreements about competitive positioning, proof selection, and value claims.

A storyboard session forces those arguments into the open early, when they're cheap to resolve. The document can hold full scripts, conversation frameworks, alternative language, and dissenting opinions in ways a slide deck cannot. Only once the narrative decisions are stable does it make sense to compress them into a visual format.

## Who Should Be in the Room

| Role | What They Contribute | What to Watch For |
|------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Sales (2–3 reps, including top performers) | Delivery instincts, objection patterns, buyer language | Over-indexing on claims that close individual deals rather than all deals |
| Marketing | Category narrative, messaging consistency, positioning alignment | Messaging that sounds good internally but lands flat externally |
| Product | Capability accuracy, roadmap constraints, technical differentiation | Hedging on differentiation claims out of engineering caution |
| Customer Success | Real proof assets, post-sale reality, common failure modes | Surfacing implementation complexity that sales hasn't been naming |
| Founder or executive | Credibility validation, category-level vision | Overweighting vision over current product reality |

If customer success isn't in the room, proof selection will skew toward the cases sales remembers rather than the cases that best represent outcomes. If product isn't present, differentiation claims will drift beyond what the product currently delivers.

**Session size:** Six to eight people is the working maximum. Larger groups turn storyboard sessions into presentations rather than working sessions.

## The Storyboard Session: Agenda and Facilitation Guide

Plan for a full working day, broken into two four-hour blocks. Don't try to compress this into a half-day — the quality of decisions made in the final two hours of a rushed session is noticeably lower than those made with adequate time.

### Block One: Setup Components (Components 1–3)

**Duration:** 3.5 hours plus 30-minute break

The Setup is the hardest part to write well and the most commonly skipped in practice. Protect this time.

#### Component 1: Market Insight (60 minutes)

Start by collecting raw material. Ask the room to surface every recurring observation they have about buyers in this space — what surprises them, what they consistently see going wrong before buyers come to you, what questions signal a buyer is actually ready to move.

Write every observation on a whiteboard without editing. Then apply three filters:

1. **Is it specific?** "Companies struggle with data" fails. "Mid-market logistics companies running SAP typically have a 6-week lag between operational data and management reporting — and the lag is in the extraction layer, not the analysis layer" passes.
2. **Could a competitor say this?** If yes, it's not a market insight; it's a category observation. Keep going.
3. **Does it connect to this buyer's current situation?** Some insights are true but irrelevant. If the buyer isn't experiencing what the insight describes, it won't land as credible — it will land as a script.

Select two or three insights that survive all three filters. Draft script language for each. The output of this step is 2–3 scripted insight paragraphs, each 3–5 sentences.

**Facilitator prompt:** "What do we know about how buyers in this space get into trouble, that they probably don't know about themselves when they first talk to us?"

#### Component 2: Alternatives (75 minutes)

Map the full alternative landscape before drafting any language. This is not a competitive teardown; it's an honest inventory.

**Landscape mapping template:**

| Alternative | Who it's right for | Genuine strengths | Where it runs into friction | Our relationship to this option |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Direct competitor A | | | | |
| Direct competitor B | | | | |
| Adjacent/DIY approach | | | | |
| Do nothing / status quo | | | | |
| Build in-house | | | | |

Fill in every cell before moving to language. Sales will resist filling in "genuine strengths" for competitors — push them. If you can't articulate why a competitor wins deals, you haven't thought clearly enough about differentiation.

The "do nothing" row deserves the most attention in most B2B software pitches. Fill it in as if it were a real product you were evaluating: what does it cost, what does it deliver, where does it fail, and when does it make sense?

Once the map is complete, draft a conversation framework — not a script. The alternatives discussion should feel like a guided conversation, not a recitation. Write three to five conversation prompts that a rep can use to move through this section.

**Facilitator prompt:** "If our best prospect decided not to buy from anyone, what would happen? Put a dollar figure and a timeline on it."

#### Component 3: Perfect World (45 minutes)

This component describes what an ideal solution would look like for a buyer in this situation — before naming your product. It should read as a set of criteria the buyer would come up with themselves if they were building an evaluation framework from scratch.

Write this as a list of questions a rep can use to prompt the buyer to articulate their own criteria, followed by a summary paragraph that synthesizes what you've heard.

**Template:**
- "If you were designing the perfect solution for your situation, what would it need to do that your current approach doesn't?"
- "What would you need to see to feel confident this was the right choice?"
- "Are there constraints — integration requirements, deployment model, team size — that any solution would have to work within?"

The summary paragraph that synthesizes the buyer's answers is where you bridge to the Introduction. Draft several versions — the language needs to feel like it's reflecting the buyer's input, not leading them.

### Block Two: Follow-Through Components (Components 4–8)

**Duration:** 3.5 hours plus 30-minute break

#### Component 4: Introduction (30 minutes)

This is the moment you name your product. It should feel like a logical conclusion from the Setup, not a pivot.

Draft two versions:
- **Short form (2–3 sentences):** For buyers who confirmed alignment with the Setup quickly
- **Extended form (5–7 sentences):** For buyers who needed more exploration of the alternatives

The introduction should name the market category you're in, identify where you sit in the landscape you just described, and make it feel like the right moment to take a closer look.

#### Component 5: Differentiated Value (75 minutes)

This is where most storyboard sessions hit their first serious disagreement. Product and sales frequently have different views on what's actually differentiated. Marketing often has language that sounds differentiated but doesn't survive scrutiny.

Run the "so what / compared to what" process on every proposed value claim. Write each claim on the whiteboard, then have the room challenge it with both questions until the claim is either specific enough to survive or is discarded.

**Working example:**

| Draft Claim | After "So What?" | After "Compared to What?" | Final Claim |
|-------------|-----------------|--------------------------|-------------|
| "Faster deployment" | "You reach value before your fiscal quarter closes" | "Competitors average 12 weeks to first workflow; we average 9 days because we handle data migration" | "Nine-day time to first workflow — we handle the migration so your team doesn't spend engineering cycles on it" |
| "Better integrations" | "Your existing tools keep working" | "Point-to-point vs. our pre-built connectors — but competitors are adding connectors too" | Discard — not differentiated enough |
| "Purpose-built for regulated industries" | "Compliance is built in, not bolted on" | "General-purpose tools require custom compliance configuration that requires a consultant; we ship with SOC 2 and HIPAA defaults" | "Ships with SOC 2 and HIPAA compliance defaults — no consultant required, no custom configuration" |

Target three to five differentiation claims that survive this process. More than five and you're not differentiating — you're listing features.

For each surviving claim, draft:
1. A one-sentence scripted version for use in conversation
2. A visual (chart, diagram, or before/after) concept for the deck

#### Component 6: Proof (45 minutes)

Map available proof assets against the claims from Component 5 and the buyer profile you're building this pitch for.

**Proof asset inventory:**

| Claim | Available Proof | Buyer Match | Gap |
|-------|----------------|-------------|-----|
| Nine-day deployment | Customer A story, average data from 20 deployments | Mid-market SaaS ✓ | Need a manufacturing example |
| SOC 2/HIPAA defaults | Healthcare customer B quote, audit documentation | Healthcare ✓ | Weak for financial services |
| [Claim 3] | | | |

Every gap in the rightmost column is a content brief for customer success or marketing. Don't move to slides until gaps are identified — you'll either need to fill them before launch or decide which buyer segments this version of the pitch serves.

#### Component 7: Objection Handling (30 minutes)

Draw from the combined experience of sales and customer success in the room. List every objection that has derailed or delayed a deal in the past six months. Organize them by type:

| Objection Type | Example | Honest Response | What Not to Say |
|---------------|---------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Price | "You're more expensive than X" | Acknowledge price difference; connect to cost of deployment lag or total cost of ownership | "We're actually cheaper when you factor in…" (before you've heard their situation) |
| Timing | "We're not ready to evaluate this quarter" | Name what "ready" would look like; explore what would change | "We can be flexible on timing" (then you've lost urgency) |
| Incumbent | "We already have a contract with Y" | Acknowledge sunk cost reality; ask what's driving the conversation despite the contract | Attacking Y directly |
| Technical | "Our engineering team will want to evaluate the API" | Welcome it; offer specific resources and a technical contact | "Our API is great, trust us" |
| Risk | "We've had bad experiences with implementations like this" | Name the specific failure pattern; explain what you do differently | Dismissing the concern |

The output is a conversation framework, not a script. Objection handling that's over-scripted sounds rehearsed; it needs room to be responsive.

#### Component 8: The Ask (15 minutes)

Define the specific next step this pitch is designed to produce. One step. Be precise.

| Context | Weak Ask | Strong Ask |
|---------|----------|------------|
| First meeting | "Let's stay in touch" | "Can we schedule a technical session with your team for next Thursday? I'll send a prep guide ahead of time." |
| After demo | "Let me know if you have questions" | "The logical next step is a reference call with a customer in your situation — I'd like to set that up for this week if you're open to it." |
| Late-stage evaluation | "We hope to earn your business" | "To move to contract, we need sign-off from you and your security lead. Can we get both on a call before the 15th?" |

## Testing the Storyboard Before Any Prospect Sees It

### Internal Delivery Test

Before showing the storyboard to any prospect, run delivery tests with sales reps who were not in the storyboard session. This surfaces two different problems:

1. **Language authenticity issues:** Scripts that were written by committee often sound like committee writing. If a rep can't deliver a sentence naturally after two attempts, rewrite it.
2. **Knowledge gaps:** If reps can't answer follow-up questions about an alternatives claim or a proof data point, the storyboard needs more supporting material.

Run at least three internal delivery tests. After each one, revise before the next. Track which sections consistently cause stumbling — that's the signal that the content, not the rep, needs work.

### Storyboard Review Checklist

| Check | Pass Criteria | Fail Signal |
|-------|--------------|-------------|
| Market Insight specificity | Insight names a pattern specific to our customer experience | Could appear in any competitor's pitch |
| Alternatives honesty | Genuine competitor strengths are named | Every alternative is framed as inferior |
| Perfect World neutrality | Criteria could lead to multiple solutions | Criteria read as a product spec for our tool |
| Differentiation precision | Each claim names a comparison point | Claims are generic outcomes without a "compared to what" |
| Proof match | Each claim has matched proof for the target buyer segment | Proof library is generic or mismatched by industry/size |
| Objection completeness | Top five deal-stalling objections have honest responses | Responses avoid the substance of hard objections |
| Ask clarity | Next step is specific, dated, and low-friction | Ask is vague or requires buyer to initiate follow-up |
| Script naturalness | Reps can deliver scripted sections without visible effort | Language stumbles or sounds rehearsed |
| Cross-functional agreement | Product, marketing, and sales agree all claims are accurate | Any team disputes any claim |
| Positioning alignment | Pitch matches current marketing positioning | Pitch and website tell different differentiation stories |

## Revision Cadence

Expect three to five rounds before the storyboard feels solid. Here's what typically surfaces in each round:

| Round | What You'll Find | What You'll Fix |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Vague differentiation claims, mismatched proof | Run "so what / compared to what" on claims; identify proof gaps |
| 2 | Language that sounds unnatural in delivery | Rewrite scripted sections closer to how reps actually speak |
| 3 | Components that work individually but don't connect | Add bridge language between components; check narrative flow |
| 4 | Setup-to-Follow-Through disconnect | Ensure the Introduction explicitly references what was established in the Setup |
| 5 | Minor calibration on objection handling and the ask | Test with buyers in lower-stakes conversations before high-stakes ones |

## Anti-Patterns in Storyboard Sessions

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Happens | Fix |
|-------------|---------------|-----|
| Jumping to slide design mid-session | Visual tools are comfortable; narrative decisions feel abstract | Ban slide software for the duration of the storyboard session |
| Letting one voice dominate alternatives | Sales culture often rewards competitive attack; honest characterization feels like weakness | Facilitator explicitly asks for genuine competitor strengths; product or marketing can often provide this more fairly |
| Writing aspirational differentiation | Founders and product managers sometimes pitch the roadmap rather than the current product | Ask: "Can a rep demonstrate this claim in a live demo today?" If no, it goes in a "near-term" section, not the main claim |
| Skipping the "do nothing" alternative | Teams feel it's not a real competitor | Frame it explicitly: inaction is the opponent who wins 40–60% of deals |
| Treating the storyboard as a one-time exercise | Markets shift, products ship, proof assets accumulate | Set a quarterly review date before you leave the storyboard session |
| No owner assigned | Storyboards produced by committee get revised by committee — slowly | Assign one owner who has final edit authority and a 30-day revision deadline |

## Maintaining the Storyboard Over Time

A storyboard built today is not the storyboard you need in six months. Assign one owner — typically a sales enablement lead, head of marketing, or a senior seller — with explicit responsibility for:

- Reviewing the storyboard after any significant product release
- Updating proof assets when new case studies are available
- Running delivery tests when new reps are onboarded
- Scheduling a formal cross-functional review every quarter

The storyboard also serves as the source document for adjacent assets: demo scripts, email sequences, content briefs, and competitive battlecards. When the storyboard is updated, downstream assets need to follow. Maintain a simple dependency map so the owner knows what else needs updating.

## Summary

The pitch storyboard is a discipline more than a document. Its value is in the cross-functional conversation it forces — the argument between sales and product about what's actually differentiated, the push from customer success for proof that reflects reality, the marketing check on whether claims align with positioning. Those conversations, held early and in the open, produce pitches that hold together under buyer scrutiny. Run the session with the right people, protect the Setup phase from being rushed, test delivery before testing with prospects, and treat revision as the expected path rather than a sign something went wrong.
