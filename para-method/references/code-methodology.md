# CODE Methodology

CODE — Capture, Organize, Distill, Express — is the operational workflow of the Second Brain. PARA tells you where information lives. CODE tells you what to do with it as it moves through your system. Each step builds on the previous one, and the cycle repeats as your creative output generates new inputs worth capturing.

---

## Capture: The Entry Point

### The Three Capture Criteria

Not everything deserves a place in your Second Brain. Forte's three filters prevent the system from drowning in noise:

**1. Does it resonate?**
Resonance is a felt sense — something stops you in your tracks, provokes a reaction, or makes you think differently. Trust that reaction. You may not know why it resonates yet, but your subconscious pattern-matching is ahead of your conscious analysis.

- A customer quote that surprises you
- A data point that contradicts your assumption
- A framework that reframes a problem you've been struggling with
- A competitive move that makes you uncomfortable

**2. Is it useful?**
Can you see a concrete future use? Not "this might be useful someday" (that's hoarding) but "I can see this being relevant to [specific project, area, or interest]."

- A template for a deliverable you create regularly
- Data that supports or challenges a decision you're making
- A process that solves a recurring operational problem
- Evidence for a case you're building

**3. Is it surprising?**
Does this challenge your existing beliefs or present something genuinely new? Information that confirms what you already know has diminishing value. Surprising information has the highest future utility because it reshapes your mental models.

- A research finding that contradicts conventional wisdom in your field
- A successful approach from an adjacent industry you haven't considered
- Customer behavior that doesn't match your persona models
- A failure mode you hadn't anticipated

### What NOT to Capture

| Type | Why to Skip | Exception |
|------|------------|-----------|
| Information that confirms what you already know | No learning value; adds noise | Unless you need it as evidence for a stakeholder pitch |
| Entire articles or documents | You're deferring the thinking to later; you'll never revisit the full text | Capture your highlights and reactions, not the source material |
| Aspirational content ("I should learn about...") | Aspiration without action creates guilt, not knowledge | Only if you're starting a specific project or learning initiative |
| Transient information (meeting logistics, status updates) | Expires quickly; clutters your system | Capture decisions and rationale from meetings, not the logistics |
| Content you can easily find again via search | Your Second Brain is for processed thinking, not bookmarks | Unless your processing (highlights, annotations) adds significant value over the raw source |

### Capture Tools and Workflows

The specific tool doesn't matter as long as it reduces friction to near zero. If capturing requires more than two steps, you won't do it consistently.

**Capture sources for PMs:**

| Source | What to Capture | How to Capture |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| Customer interviews | Surprising quotes, unexpected behaviors, new patterns | Interview snapshot immediately after; highlight top 3-5 moments |
| Books and articles | Key frameworks, specific examples, data points that resonate | Highlight while reading; export highlights to notes app |
| Meetings | Decisions with rationale, commitments, insights from others | 2-3 bullet summary within 5 minutes of meeting end |
| Slack and email | Decisions, insights from cross-functional partners, requests with context | Forward or save to notes app; add 1-sentence context |
| Conferences and talks | One or two novel ideas per session, specific examples | Quick capture during talk; distill to key takeaways within 24 hours |
| Your own thinking | Ideas that come during walks, showers, commutes | Voice memo or quick note; process during Weekly Review |

### The Capture-Organization Gap

Forte's critical principle: **separate capture from organization.** In the moment of capture, your goal is to save the material with minimal friction. Organization happens later, during dedicated review time. Trying to organize while capturing interrupts both processes — you lose the thought while searching for the right folder.

**The workflow:**

1. Capture into a single inbox (one inbox, not one per tool)
2. During Weekly Review, process inbox items into PARA categories
3. Add minimal context at capture time: a sentence about why you saved it

### Copy Patterns

- "Does this resonate, is it useful, or is it surprising? If none, skip it."
- "Save it to the inbox now; I'll organize during my weekly review."
- "I'd save this 'just in case' — which means I shouldn't save it."

**Ethical boundary:** Capture for your own learning and output. Do not capture proprietary or confidential information from previous employers, clients, or partners in ways that violate agreements. Your Second Brain should enhance your thinking, not serve as an unauthorized knowledge transfer mechanism.

## Organize: Placing Information Where It's Useful

### The Organizing Question

Every piece of information gets one question: **"In which project, area, or resource will this be most useful?"**

Not: "What topic is this about?" (librarian thinking)
Not: "Where does this logically belong?" (taxonomist thinking)
Not: "Where would I look for this later?" (archivist thinking)

The question is always about future use in an active context.

### The Cathedral Effect

Forte borrows this concept from environmental psychology: high ceilings and grand spaces promote abstract, creative thinking. Low ceilings and constrained spaces promote detail-oriented, focused work. Your digital environment has the same effect. A cluttered, poorly organized workspace promotes cluttered, unfocused thinking. A clean PARA structure creates the conditions for clear, purposeful work.

**Practical implications:**

| Digital Environment | Cognitive Effect | PARA Application |
|--------------------|-----------------|------------------|
| Inbox with 500 unprocessed items | Anxiety; avoidance; reactive behavior | Process to zero during Weekly Review |
| 50 active project folders | Overwhelm; inability to prioritize | Reduce to 10-12; archive or pause the rest |
| Resource folder with 200 topics | False sense of knowledge; no depth | Archive topics untouched in 90 days; go deep on 10-15 |
| Clean, current project list | Focus; clarity on priorities; intentional action | The goal state — maintained through reviews |

### Cross-Platform Consistency

PARA should be identical across every tool you use. The same four top-level categories, the same project names, the same organizational logic.

**Why this matters for PMs:**

PMs typically use 5-10 tools daily: notes app, file storage, task manager, email, Slack, documentation platform, analytics tools, design tools. If each tool has its own organizational logic, you spend cognitive cycles translating between systems. PARA provides a universal structure.

**Implementation pattern:**

```
NOTES APP (Notion/Obsidian/etc.)    FILE STORAGE (Google Drive/etc.)
├── Projects                         ├── Projects
│   ├── Q2 Strategy Memo             │   ├── Q2 Strategy Memo
│   └── Pricing Redesign             │   └── Pricing Redesign
├── Areas                            ├── Areas
│   ├── Product Quality              │   ├── Product Quality
│   └── Team Operations              │   └── Team Operations
├── Resources                        ├── Resources
│   └── Pricing Psychology           │   └── Pricing Psychology
└── Archives                         └── Archives

TASK MANAGER (Linear/Asana/etc.)    EMAIL LABELS
├── Projects                         ├── Projects
│   ├── Q2 Strategy Memo             │   ├── Q2 Strategy Memo
│   └── Pricing Redesign             │   └── Pricing Redesign
├── Areas                            ├── Areas
└── (Resources/Archives rarely       └── Archive
     needed in task manager)
```

### Organizing Decisions for Common PM Scenarios

| Scenario | Wrong Instinct | Right Approach |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| You read an article about pricing psychology | Save to "Pricing" topic folder | Ask: "Am I working on a pricing project right now?" If yes, put it in that project. If no, put it in Resources: "Pricing Psychology" |
| A colleague shares a useful competitive analysis template | Save to "Templates" folder | Ask: "Which project or area needs this?" If your active project is a competitive analysis, put it there. Otherwise, Area: "Market Awareness" |
| You take notes during a quarterly business review | Save to "Meeting Notes" by date | Ask: "Which project do these notes inform?" Probably your Q2 planning project — put them there |
| You find a great framework for stakeholder mapping | Save to "Frameworks" resource | Ask: "Do I have an active project that needs stakeholder mapping?" If yes, project folder. If ongoing responsibility, Area. If general interest, Resource |

### Organizing Principles in Practice

| Context | Organization Principle | Example |
|---------|----------------------|---------|
| New project kickoff | Create the project folder across all platforms simultaneously | New "Q2 Strategy" project gets a folder in Notes, Drive, and Jira |
| Incoming information | Ask "Where will I use this?" not "What is this about?" | A competitive insight goes in the "Board Presentation" project, not in "Competitive Intel" resource |
| Tool sprawl | Apply PARA consistently across all tools | Same four top-level categories in Notion, Google Drive, email labels, and bookmark folders |

### Copy Patterns

- "Where will I actually use this next? File it there."
- "Move it into the project folder; one note, one location."
- "If I can't picture using it, I don't need to organize it — archive or delete."

## Distill: Finding the Essence

Distillation is covered in depth in [progressive-summarization.md](progressive-summarization.md). The key principles from the CODE perspective:

### Distillation as Thinking

Distillation is not summarization — it's a form of thinking. When you bold the most important passages in a note, you're making a judgment about what matters. When you write a Layer 4 executive summary, you're synthesizing multiple ideas into a coherent insight. Each layer of distillation deepens your understanding of the material.

### When to Distill

| Trigger | Distillation Action | Layer |
|---------|-------------------|-------|
| Capturing a new note | Add minimal context: one sentence about why you saved it | Layer 1 |
| Revisiting a note for an active project | Bold the passages most relevant to your current work | Layer 2 |
| Using a note to inform a decision | Highlight the core insights within the bold | Layer 3 |
| Making a note a permanent reference | Write a 2-3 sentence summary at the top in your own words | Layer 4 |
| Producing a deliverable | Combine distilled notes into new output | Layer 5 |

### The Distillation Mistake PMs Make

PMs are trained to be thorough. The instinct is to capture everything and distill nothing — keeping full interview transcripts, complete competitive reports, and exhaustive meeting notes. This creates a system where retrieval requires re-reading entire documents. Progressive summarization solves this by creating a gradient from detail to essence, making every note scannable at multiple levels of depth.

### Copy Patterns

- "Add a two-sentence summary at the top before I close this note."
- "Bold the parts that survive a second read; highlight the parts that survive a third."
- "I don't need to distill this yet — only if a project pulls it in."

## Express: The Purpose of the System

### Output as the Goal

This is the most important and most neglected step. The entire Second Brain exists to improve your creative output. If you're capturing, organizing, and distilling but not producing better work, the system is a sophisticated hobby, not a productivity tool.

**Forte's key insight:** Most people think of creativity as generating ideas from nothing. In practice, creativity is combining existing ideas in new ways. A Second Brain provides the raw material for combination. Intermediate packets — distilled notes, outtakes, work-in-process, and past deliverables — are the building blocks of creative output.

### The Express Workflow for PMs

| Deliverable | Before Second Brain | With Second Brain |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Strategy memo | Start with blank page; research from scratch; 2-3 days | Assemble from IPs: distilled customer insights, competitive analysis, strategic frameworks; 4-6 hours |
| Board presentation | Rebuild narrative each quarter; hunt for data; 1-2 days | Update existing IP template; pull in new data from project folders; 2-4 hours |
| PRD | Write from scratch; re-derive context; 1-2 days | Reuse standard sections (IPs); pull from active project research folder; 3-5 hours |
| Stakeholder pitch | Reconstruct argument each time; find supporting evidence; half day | Remix Setup narrative (IP) with current project evidence; 1-2 hours |
| Team onboarding doc | Compile from memory and scattered docs; full day | Assemble from Area folders: team norms, decision history, stakeholder map IPs; 2-3 hours |
| External content (blog posts, conference talks, thought leadership) | Generate ideas and evidence from scratch | Resources folder provides curated raw material; progressive summarization surfaces the best ideas |

### Copy Patterns

- "What's the deliverable this is going to feed?"
- "Before I draft from scratch, what IPs from past projects can I assemble?"
- "Ship a rough version this week; the Second Brain only earns its keep when it produces output."

### The Feedback Loop

Express feeds back into Capture. The process of creating output surfaces gaps in your knowledge ("I need data on X"), generates new insights ("Combining A and B reveals C"), and produces deliverables that become Intermediate Packets for future projects. This is the virtuous cycle that makes a Second Brain a compounding asset rather than a static archive.

```
CAPTURE  ──►  ORGANIZE  ──►  DISTILL  ──►  EXPRESS
   ▲                                           │
   │         New insights, gaps identified,     │
   │         deliverables become future IPs     │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## CODE as a Daily Practice

| Time of Day | CODE Activity | Duration |
|-------------|--------------|----------|
| Morning | Review active project folders; identify today's Express output | 5-10 min |
| Throughout the day | Capture as you go: quick notes, saved links, voice memos | 1-2 min per capture |
| End of day | Quick organize: move captured items from inbox to PARA categories | 5-10 min |
| When revisiting notes | Distill: add bold, highlights, or summaries as you use notes | 2-5 min per note |
| Dedicated work blocks | Express: produce deliverables by assembling IPs and distilled notes | 30-120 min |
| Weekly Review (Friday) | Full cycle: process inbox (Capture → Organize), distill active notes, extract IPs (Express) | 30-60 min |

The system works not because any single step is powerful, but because the cycle compounds. Each rotation through CODE makes the next rotation faster and produces higher-quality output.
