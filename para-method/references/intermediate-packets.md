# Intermediate Packets

Intermediate Packets (IPs) are the building blocks of creative output in the Second Brain system. An IP is any discrete, reusable unit of work product — a distilled note, an analysis outtake, a draft section, a completed deliverable, or a template — that can be combined and recombined across projects. IPs are how a Second Brain generates compounding returns: work done for one project accelerates every future project that touches the same material.

---

## Why Intermediate Packets Matter

### The Blank Page Problem

Most knowledge work starts from a blank page. A PM needs to write a strategy memo, a PRD, a competitive analysis, or a board presentation. The default approach: sit down, open a blank document, and generate everything from scratch — research, structure, argument, evidence, prose.

This approach has three problems:

1. **It's slow.** Generating everything from scratch takes days for major deliverables.
2. **It's anxiety-inducing.** A blank page creates a psychological barrier that leads to procrastination.
3. **It wastes past work.** You've already done research, written analyses, and produced deliverables on related topics. Starting from scratch ignores all of it.

### The Assembly Model

With IPs, the workflow shifts from generation to assembly. A strategy memo isn't written from nothing — it's assembled from:

- A distilled competitive analysis (IP from a past project)
- Customer interview summaries (IPs from ongoing discovery)
- A strategic framework (IP from a book or article)
- Metric dashboards and data visualizations (IPs from your analytics area)
- A section structure (IP from a past strategy memo)

The final deliverable requires original synthesis and new writing, but it starts 40-60% complete. The heavy lifting was done incrementally, spread across weeks or months of capture and distillation.

## The Five Types of Intermediate Packets

### 1. Distilled Notes

Key takeaways from research, reading, interviews, or conversations — processed through progressive summarization to at least Layer 2-3.

**Examples for PMs:**
- A one-page synthesis of 8 customer interviews on onboarding pain points
- Highlighted key findings from an industry report on your market segment
- A framework summary from a product management book with your annotations on how it applies to your context

**How to create:** Apply progressive summarization during capture and review. The Layer 3-4 version of a note is a ready-made IP.

### 2. Outtakes

Material created for a project that didn't make the final cut — alternative analyses, dropped sections, explored-but-rejected approaches. Outtakes are often more valuable than the final output because they represent thinking that was done but not yet deployed.

**Examples for PMs:**
- Three pricing models you evaluated for a pricing project, of which only one was recommended
- A competitive positioning section you cut from a board presentation because of time constraints
- An alternative product strategy direction you explored but didn't propose

**How to create:** When cutting material from a deliverable, save it as a separate note in the project folder. Tag it as an outtake. When the project archives, the outtake goes with it — retrievable for future projects.

### 3. Work-in-Process

Drafts, outlines, partially completed deliverables, and structured thinking that isn't finished but has significant value.

**Examples for PMs:**
- A half-written PRD for a feature that got deprioritized
- An outline for a strategy presentation you started but didn't finish
- A structured list of assumptions for a feature idea you haven't tested yet

**How to create:** Save early and often. Any structured thinking — even an outline or a list of questions — is a work-in-process IP. It's faster to resume a half-finished draft than to start from scratch.

### 4. Final Deliverables

Completed outputs from past projects. These are the most obvious IPs but also the most underused, because people file them away and never look at them again.

**Examples for PMs:**
- Last quarter's strategy memo
- A shipped PRD
- A completed competitive analysis
- A board presentation deck
- A retrospective document

**How to create:** Already created — the key is to archive them in a retrievable location and extract reusable components before archiving.

### 5. Documents from Others

Frameworks, templates, and reference materials from colleagues, mentors, or external sources that you've found valuable enough to save.

**Examples for PMs:**
- A stakeholder mapping template from your director
- An industry benchmark report shared by a colleague
- A decision-making framework from a conference talk
- A well-written PRD from another PM that serves as a structural model

**How to create:** Capture when someone shares something valuable. Add a note about why you saved it and where you might use it.

## The Project Completion Protocol

The most valuable moment for IP creation is when a project completes. This is when you have maximum context and the work is fresh. The protocol:

### Step-by-Step

| Step | Action | Time | Output |
|------|--------|------|--------|
| 1 | Review all project deliverables | 10 min | List of completed outputs |
| 2 | Identify reusable components | 10 min | List of potential IPs |
| 3 | Extract IPs as standalone notes | 15 min | 3-7 IPs saved with context |
| 4 | Apply Layer 2-3 progressive summarization to each IP | 10 min | Distilled, scannable IPs |
| 5 | File IPs in appropriate PARA categories | 5 min | IPs in Areas or Resources for future retrieval |
| 6 | Archive the full project folder | 5 min | Clean project space |

### What to Extract

| Project Element | Extractable IP | Future Use |
|----------------|---------------|------------|
| Research synthesis | Distilled findings document | Next project in the same domain |
| Decision rationale | Decision memo with criteria and trade-offs | Similar decisions in future projects |
| Process or methodology | Documented approach that worked (or didn't) | Repeating the process; onboarding others |
| Templates and structures | Reusable document structures, slide formats | Faster creation of similar deliverables |
| Stakeholder feedback | What resonated, what was challenged, how objections were handled | Preparing for similar stakeholder conversations |
| Metrics and outcomes | Before/after data, success metrics, actual results | Evidence for future proposals; calibrating estimates |

### Worked Example: Completing a Pricing Restructure Project

**Project outputs:**
- Pricing recommendation memo (final deliverable)
- Three pricing model analyses (two outtakes, one selected)
- Customer interview summaries on price sensitivity (distilled notes)
- Revenue impact projections spreadsheet (analysis artifact)
- Board presentation on pricing changes (final deliverable)
- Post-launch pricing metrics (outcomes data)

**Extracted IPs:**

| IP | Type | Filed In | Future Use |
|----|------|----------|------------|
| Pricing model comparison framework | Outtake + Template | Resource: "Pricing Strategy" | Next pricing decision |
| Customer price sensitivity synthesis (5 interviews distilled) | Distilled notes | Area: "Customer Research" | Any future pricing or packaging project |
| Revenue impact projection template | Template | Resource: "Financial Modeling" | Any project requiring revenue projections |
| Board presentation: pricing narrative section | Final deliverable section | Area: "Stakeholder Communication" | Future board presentations on pricing topics |
| Lessons learned: what worked in the stakeholder alignment process | Process IP | Area: "Stakeholder Relationships" | Next cross-functional initiative requiring executive buy-in |

## Building an IP Library Over Time

### The Compounding Effect

| Time Horizon | IP Library State | Deliverable Speed |
|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Month 1 | Sparse; most deliverables still start from scratch | Minimal improvement |
| Month 3 | 20-30 IPs accumulated; some reuse across projects | 20-30% faster on deliverables related to past projects |
| Month 6 | 50-80 IPs; coverage across most common deliverable types | 40-50% faster; most deliverables start partially complete |
| Year 1 | 100-150 IPs; rich library across projects, areas, and resources | 50-60% faster; major deliverables feel like assembly, not creation |
| Year 2+ | 200+ IPs; deep coverage; IPs reference and build on each other | Qualitative shift — new projects benefit from the compound knowledge of all past projects |

### IP Maintenance

| Review Cadence | Action | Purpose |
|---------------|--------|---------|
| Weekly | Tag new IPs created during the week | Keeps the library current |
| Monthly | Review IPs used this month; apply deeper progressive summarization | Most-used IPs get most distilled |
| Quarterly | Audit IP library; archive stale IPs; identify gaps | Ensures the library reflects your current work |

## Common Mistakes with Intermediate Packets

| Mistake | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Skipping the extraction step at project completion | All project-specific work becomes unretrievable once archived | Block 30 minutes after every project completion for IP extraction |
| Creating IPs that are too large (entire documents) | Not reusable because they contain too much project-specific context | Extract the reusable component — a section, a framework, a data set — not the entire deliverable |
| Creating IPs that are too small (single facts or quotes) | Not substantial enough to be building blocks | IPs should be self-contained units of thinking — a synthesis, an analysis, a framework application — not raw data points |
| Not adding context to IPs | "Pricing analysis" with no context about when, why, or for whom | Add a 1-2 sentence header: what this is, when it was created, and what it's useful for |
| Hoarding IPs without using them | Library grows but output quality doesn't improve | Before starting any deliverable, search for relevant IPs first; make "check for existing IPs" the first step of any project |
| Only saving your own work | Missing valuable frameworks, templates, and examples from others | Capture documents from others that pass the three capture criteria; attribute them properly |

---

## Summary

Intermediate Packets transform knowledge work from a series of isolated, start-from-scratch efforts into a cumulative, compounding practice. The five types — distilled notes, outtakes, work-in-process, final deliverables, and documents from others — cover the full range of reusable work product. The Project Completion Protocol ensures that every finished project feeds the IP library. Over months and years, the library grows into a formidable asset that makes every new deliverable faster, higher quality, and more thoroughly grounded in past learning. The PM who builds an IP library for a year will consistently outperform the PM who starts fresh every quarter.
